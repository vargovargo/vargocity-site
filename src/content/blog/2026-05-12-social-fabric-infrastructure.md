*On what holds communities together — and what it would take to measure it.*

---

There are neighborhoods where the heat kills you and neighborhoods where it doesn't. Same city, same summer, same outdoor temperatures. In Chicago in 1995, the mortality gap between adjacent blocks wasn't explained by income or age or building stock. It was explained by whether people knew each other — whether the social infrastructure of a neighborhood was intact enough that someone checked in on the elderly woman upstairs, or noticed the man next door hadn't opened his window in two days.

Eric Klinenberg called these places "palaces for the people." Libraries, community centers, barbershops, parks, walkable streets — the spaces where incidental contact happens, where civic life keeps running, where residents are enough of a community to function as one in a crisis. His argument, backed by epidemiological data, was that the physical layout of social life is an infrastructure like any other. It can be built. It can be let to decay. When it decays, people die alone.

---

Robert Putnam was tracking a related phenomenon at the same time. By 2000, he'd documented decades of civic decline — not cultural rot, but something structural. The density of civic organizations, the rate of participation, the strength of the relationships that make collective action possible had all been in sustained decline for thirty years. His research identified one of the clearest predictors: each additional ten minutes of commuting reduces civic engagement by roughly ten percent. That's a rate, not a rough correlation — and it compounds. The person driving forty-five minutes each way is substantially less likely to know their neighbors, join a local organization, or vote in a municipal election than someone with a fifteen-minute commute.

This isn't personality. It's a built environment producing a social outcome. The car-dependent sprawl that restructured American geography after World War II didn't just shape where people lived. It shaped how often and how deeply they encountered each other. Sprawl isn't a land-use problem that happens to have social consequences. It's a social intervention — implemented at continental scale, without a vote.

---

What Klinenberg and Putnam are both reaching toward — from epidemiology and political science, respectively — is that social capital is itself an infrastructure. Not a cultural inheritance, not a demographic fixed effect. The connective tissue that routes information, coordinates collective action, and holds communities together in a crisis. Like roads or water systems: something built, something that can be let to decay, something that fails visibly when it does.

Klinenberg's contribution is to show that this infrastructure depends on physical space to function — that the layout of a neighborhood is also the layout of its social life. What neither fully names is a third layer that has undergone rapid, widespread collapse across thousands of American communities in the past twenty years.

The local newspaper is an informational palace for the people. It's where shared local reality gets constructed — where residents learn what the city council voted, what the zoning board approved, what the hospital is planning. Where civic life becomes legible to the people it affects. When it closes, what replaces it isn't nothing: it's individually personalized feeds, engineered for engagement, indifferent to place — and indifferent to shared reality at any scale. Walter Cronkite was nationally optimized, not locally. But he created something everyone in the room watched together. The algorithm doesn't even do that. It disaggregates the picture into as many versions as there are viewers, isolating people inside information that has nothing in common with their neighbors'.

More than 2,500 local news outlets have closed since 2005. The loss is concentrated in communities already losing physical social infrastructure — places where commutes got longer, third places closed, libraries cut hours. Both forms of erosion are products of the same economic geography. They compound in communities that can least absorb the loss. That compounding is almost entirely unmeasured.

---

Putnam's longer arc offers a frame that's useful for more than diagnosis. *The Upswing* traces the early twentieth century's sustained rebuilding of civic life — not spontaneously, but through deliberate institutional construction. Progressive Era reformers built libraries, settlement houses, civic organizations, and mechanisms of democratic accountability. The half-century of rising civic participation that followed is partly the story of those investments.

The Progressive Era reformers didn't wait for the problem to be legible at scale before they started building. That excuse isn't available now. For the first time, the aggregate picture is visible — county by county, dimension by dimension — and that changes what deliberate construction looks like.

---

Making that compounding visible is the first piece. From public administrative records — civic organization density, library reach, nonprofit infrastructure, the institutions where people can still show up — it's possible to build a county-level picture of where the social fabric is intact and where it's thinned. The same way Walk Score made walkability legible for cities, street by street: a number that lets communities see the condition of their own infrastructure, and planners see where investment changes the picture.

The second is estimating what the collapse of the informational layer actually does to the social infrastructure underneath it. Newspaper closures didn't happen everywhere at once — they arrived in different communities at different times, which creates the conditions to isolate the effect. Compare what happens in places that lost their local paper to similar places that didn't, before and after the closure, controlling for everything else that was changing. The picture that emerges is the compounding Klinenberg documented in one Chicago summer, operating at national scale, in the communities least equipped to absorb it.

The research described here will attempt to answer the question of what builds these infrastructures back — and where, and in what order.
