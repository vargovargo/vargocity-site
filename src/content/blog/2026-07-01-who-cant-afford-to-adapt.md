*On what the three shapes of heat each reveal about who adaptation is designed for — and who it misses.*

---

Post 2 in this series made the within-county argument: inside a county, the heat wave is shared and the suffering isn't. The warehouse worker and the remote manager are on the same hot day, in the same county, and they are not in the same situation.

This post is at a different scale. Which counties face the worst combination of exposure and adaptive capacity? And what does that actually look like — because the answer changes depending on which shape the heat pressure takes. Shock, stress, and shift each carry a different equity problem. The places where those problems are worst are not the same places. And whether the adaptation that exists reaches those places depends on getting that distinction right.

## In shock places: does the new infrastructure reach who died?

Shock-shaped adaptation, as Post 3 described for Clackamas County and the Pacific Northwest, is about building what wasn't there. Oregon's outdoor heat rule, Cooling Portland, King County's Extreme Heat Mitigation Strategy — a substantial portfolio of new programs, regulations, and infrastructure created in the four years since the 2021 heat dome. By any standard, more than most regions would build in the same window.

The equity question in shock places is specific: does the new infrastructure reach the people who needed it most? The 2021 dome killed 72 people in Multnomah County — disproportionately older, renters, alone, in upper-floor apartments without cross-ventilation. The housing gap that made the dome lethal was distributed unevenly: rental homes in Portland run about 66 percent air conditioning penetration, against 86 percent in owner-occupied homes. Cooling Portland, now at more than 15,770 units installed, was designed precisely for that gap.

In 2022, five more people died in public and affordable housing — after the 2021 lessons, after the programs launched. Portland's indoor temperature monitoring partnership can tell you when a building gets dangerously hot. It cannot install cooling. King County's cool-kit program for unhoused residents — water, wet towels, sun protection — is named as a priority in King County's Extreme Heat Mitigation Strategy and was unfunded as of 2024. Three years after the dome, the adaptation track for the most exposed populations is slower and less resourced than the general-population track.

Shock adaptation invents new systems quickly at the regulatory and programmatic level. Getting those systems to the households that needed them before the event is its own track, moving at a different institutional speed. And the gap between those two tracks is where the equity problem lives.

## In stress places: who does the relief network miss?

The equity question in stress places is different. It's not whether infrastructure exists — in places like Phoenix, it does. It's about who the infrastructure is designed to reach.

Maricopa County's heat-death surveillance has tracked mortality since 2006, longer than almost anywhere in the country. What the data show is a concentrated pattern: heat deaths skew heavily toward people experiencing homelessness, substance use, and social isolation. These are not the populations that a cooling center map is designed to reach. Phoenix's regional network of more than 200 cooling and respite sites — professionally coordinated, 24/7 since 2024 — reaches people who can get to a building. David Hondula, director of Phoenix's Office of Heat Response and Mitigation, has described the vulnerability of these populations as one that "doesn't begin and end with heat." The adaptations scaling around them are scaling for a different risk profile.

That is the within-stress-place equity problem. The across-place problem is more systematic and more measurable.

The equity analysis runs against all 2,992 counties in the panel. The correlation between a county's stress score and its FEMA Social Vulnerability Index is +0.45. Between stress score and FEMA Community Resilience: -0.36. Stress-dominant counties have a median resilience score of 31 — against 70 for shock counties and 55 for shift counties. They have a median social vulnerability index of 76, against 41 for shock and 41 for shift. Their weighted-median household income is $68,000, against $83,000 for shock and shift counties.

The counties most urgently in need of extending their heat infrastructure past its design limits are also the least positioned to do it.

![Heat type double-burden map](/plots/heat-map-toggle.png)

Three hundred and five counties meet all three thresholds simultaneously — stress score ≥ 0.5, SoVI ≥ 70, resilience ≤ 40. The geography is not random. Madison Parish, Louisiana: stress 0.99, SoVI 99 out of 100, resilience 35, median income $34,508. East Carroll Parish: stress 0.96, SoVI 99, income $30,856. Wilkinson County, Mississippi: stress 0.95, SoVI 100, income $34,928. The top counties by stress score are overwhelmingly Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas, with Imperial County, California at the western edge — stress 0.91, SoVI 85, resilience 2, an agricultural economy built on farmworkers who labor in conditions that kill people in counties with higher scores.

Madison Parish does not have a chief heat officer. It does not have a 24/7 respite facility or a coordinated regional cooling network. It does not have the budget to retrofit its housing stock, expand emergency medical capacity, or deploy the stress-adaptation interventions that Phoenix has built over two decades. What these counties face is the hardest adaptation challenge with the fewest tools to run it. That is not incidental. It is structural.

## In shift places: who gets to be the destination?

The shift equity argument is different again, and the least visible.

Ann Arbor, Michigan — the paradigmatic shift case in this series — has spent years building for an anticipated future. A $22 million district geothermal system coming to the Bryant neighborhood. A Carbon Neutrality Plan with dedicated city funding. A Sustainable Energy Utility, approved by voters in 2024. In 2019, Missy Stults, Ann Arbor's Director of Sustainability and Innovations, received a National League of Cities grant to investigate what climate in-migration could mean for the city's infrastructure — specifically, what it implied for Ann Arbor's Capital Improvements Plan and imminent upgrades to its water treatment plant. "With people moving in," Stults said, "it has the potential to strain our infrastructure if it's not designed to increase capacity."

That is shift-shaped adaptation: investing in infrastructure capacity ahead of the demand that climate migration is expected to bring. Sizing a water treatment plant for a larger city than currently exists. Planning the land use before the pressure arrives. Building the geothermal loop in the neighborhood that is 75 percent low-income before the arrivals begin to price it out.

The equity question shift raises is which places get to do that.

Detroit, Flint, and Saginaw share the Great Lakes water advantage that makes Ann Arbor an attractive climate destination. They don't share Ann Arbor's planning capacity, its billion-dollar climate plan, or its university-town tax base. Vermont's small towns appear on every climate-haven list — fresh water, mild summers, relative safety from coastal hazard and wildfire — and are run by part-time clerks and minimally staffed road crews. "We have no county government," one Vermont regional planner told the National League of Cities. "Everything is wall-to-wall town, and our towns are all minimally staffed." To absorb the climate migration Vermont's geography invites, the state would need to invest in water infrastructure, affordable housing, and flood protection ahead of arrival. The capacity to plan for that, before the pressure peaks, is not there.

The shift opportunity accrues to places already positioned to act on it. That is a different kind of inequity than the within-county inequality Post 2 described or the double-burden counties' structural deficit — but it determines whether the places that could become durable climate destinations actually do. And it means that the climate's reorganization, left to itself, concentrates the upside in the places that were already advantaged.

---

Across all three shapes, the gap takes the same form: where adaptation is most needed and where the capacity to deliver it lives are not the same places. In shock places, the most exposed households are the last to be reached by programs designed for them. In stress places, the concentrated-vulnerability populations are outside the design envelope of the networks being scaled. In shift places, the physical advantage of a Great Lakes location or a northern climate is available to everyone, but the institutional capacity to plan around it is not.

The federal programs that bridge this gap — FEMA's Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities, CDBG-DR, the IRA's Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund — are the mechanism. Their design determines whether they reach the Madison Parishes and the Vermont towns and the affordable housing units in Portland. The National League of Cities documented the constraint plainly: communities without dedicated planning, emergency management, or sustainability staff find applying for and winning competitive grants structurally harder than those with staff. The counties at the top of the double-burden list have, by definition, the least of all three. Earmarked funding and direct technical assistance close this gap; competitive grants by themselves do not.

The next posts in this series work through what this looks like in specific domains — labor, health, energy. The gap persists across all of them. But the starting point is the same: which communities face the most acute version of the problem, and whether the adaptation that exists is designed to reach them.
