*Three counties, three shapes of heat pressure, three different conversations about what to build next — and a tool to find yours.*

---

The first two posts in this series built the case for a distinction that most climate policy still doesn't make: that a county facing its first real heat emergency is not doing the same thing as one whose cooling infrastructure is being pushed past its design limits, and neither of them is doing the same thing as a county whose entire economic geography is slowly relocating. Shock, stress, shift. Three shapes. Three different responses.

This post tries to make that concrete. The [interactive tool](/lab/heat) assigns each U.S. county a dominant type based on its projected climate trajectory, current exposure, and community context. What follows are three county sketches — one for each type — that try to show what the classification actually implies in practice. Not a scorecard. A starting point for a different conversation.

---

## Clackamas County, Oregon: shock means building what wasn't there

![Clackamas County heat typology panel](/plots/heat-panel-clackamas.png)

The tool scores Clackamas County at 77 percent shock — one of the cleaner dominant classifications in the dataset, because what happened there is one of the cleaner shock events in recent US climate history. The shock signal measures percent change in extreme heat days from a near-zero baseline: Clackamas historically averaged fewer than two days per year with heat index above 85°F, projected to grow to roughly ten. Heat as a serious hazard was effectively absent from the baseline. The dome made that absence a liability.

Clackamas County sits south of Portland — suburban in its northwest corner, quickly rural heading east, with small cities that mostly grew up around timber, agriculture, and the mild climate the Pacific Northwest has always offered. In June 2021, its northern neighborhoods recorded temperatures above 110°F. The county's emergency management plan had no heat annex. Most of its housing was built without air conditioning because the climate didn't historically require it. The older stock — much of it rental, much of it upstairs units under flat roofs — had no cross-ventilation, no AC rough-in, no way to stay cool when the outside temperature exceeded the interior even at midnight.

Shock is defined by the absence of prior design. The danger isn't that the cooling system was overwhelmed. It's that there was no cooling system to overwhelm.

The World Weather Attribution analysis found the 2021 heat dome was roughly 150 times more likely given current levels of climate change than in the pre-industrial climate — effectively impossible in the baseline. Portland hit 116°F. Seventy-two people died in Multnomah County, the deadliest natural disaster in the county's recorded history. Clackamas is the county just to the south; the event didn't respect county lines.

The four years since the dome are a natural experiment in shock-shaped adaptation — what a region builds when it discovers it needs adaptation it never built before. Three tracks have run at different institutional speeds.

The fastest was regulatory. Oregon passed its outdoor heat illness prevention rule in the months following the dome and made it permanent in May 2022 — the first state in the country to extend heat protections to indoor settings as well as outdoor. Any workplace with a heat index above 80°F is covered: manufacturing, warehousing, any setting where the air doesn't move. Oregon also passed SB 1536, emergency legislation prohibiting landlords from barring tenants from using portable cooling devices from May through September. In a stress-shaped city like Phoenix, such a law would be redundant. In Oregon, it had to be created from scratch because the prior housing regime treated air conditioning as optional — because the climate hadn't previously required it. The workforce most exposed in those early seasons — agricultural workers, warehouse workers, construction workers, largely Latino, largely without institutional power to demand protection — was also disproportionately housed in the upstairs rentals that SB 1536 was written for. The regulatory response was extraordinary by any standard. It still arrived after the people who died in it.

The second track was direct cooling deployment. The Portland Clean Energy Community Benefits Fund launched Cooling Portland in 2022, a program distributing free portable air conditioning units to low-income, heat-vulnerable residents — seniors, people with disabilities, those with heat-sensitive health conditions. By mid-2025, more than 15,770 units had been installed, exceeding the original five-year target eighteen months ahead of schedule. Rental housing in Portland has about 66 percent AC penetration versus 86 percent in owner-occupied homes. The people who died in the dome were disproportionately renters, alone, in upstairs units without cross-ventilation. Cooling Portland is, in an important sense, the adaptation program designed precisely for that distribution.

The third track is institutional and strategic, and it moves slower. King County's Extreme Heat Mitigation Strategy — the most comprehensive heat plan in the region — was released in 2024, three years after the dome. Portland's heat-resilient building code updates are in progress. Multnomah County's cooling spaces network — libraries, community centers, public buildings open during regular hours on hot days — is in place, as is TriMet's free-fare policy during heat emergencies. But the work of reaching people who can't get to a library continues. Five additional Multnomah County deaths occurred during the 2022 heat wave, after the 2021 lessons, and they were disproportionately in public and affordable housing. Portland's Bureau of Emergency Management now runs an indoor temperature monitoring partnership with the housing authority and Portland State University — sensors in affordable housing units that warn when spaces become dangerous. Monitoring tells you where it's hot. It doesn't install cooling.

Shock places have time before the next event — usually, not always — and the investment is in building what the prior assumption said you didn't need. What the PNW has built since 2021 is more than most regions would have built in the same window. The equity design question is whether the build-out reaches the people without the margin to wait — and in the Pacific Northwest, that question is not yet settled.

## Maricopa County, Arizona: stress means extending past the envelope

![Maricopa County heat typology panel](/plots/heat-panel-maricopa.png)

The tool scores Maricopa County at 86 percent stress, 14 percent shift. The stress signal is driven by absolute growth in days above 95°F heat index from an already-high baseline: nearly 98 such days per year historically, projected to grow by another 34. More days at temperatures the infrastructure already manages, for longer than it was designed to manage them. The real-world evidence confirms that classification more thoroughly than almost any other county in the country.

Maricopa County knows heat. Phoenix has been managing extreme summer temperature for decades — the built environment is organized around it. The grid is enormous, run hard, and deeply understood. Outdoor work in construction and agriculture has established schedules, shade requirements, hydration stations. The cooling center map exists and is updated annually. In 2021, Phoenix became the first city in the United States to appoint a chief heat officer: David Hondula, a heat mortality researcher who now directs the city's Office of Heat Response and Mitigation. The county health department has tracked heat-related mortality since the 1990s.

What Maricopa is discovering is that the adaptations built for the heat they had are being pushed past the assumptions they were built for.

In 2023, the county recorded 645 confirmed heat-related deaths — a number that involves real undercounting, because death certificates tend to attribute heat illness as a contributing rather than direct cause. The cooling season, once defined by four months of reliably triple-digit temperatures, has been extending into what used to be October and into what used to be April. Nights are warming faster than days. The adaptive significance of that is substantial: the body's capacity to recover from heat stress depends on nighttime temperature dropping far enough for rest. When nights stay in the mid-80s, the recovery window shrinks, and the people who don't have air conditioning in their bedrooms face cumulative heat burden across the whole summer.

The trail closures Phoenix runs during excessive heat warnings are one visible marker of how far the institutional response has had to stretch. Trailheads at South Mountain, Camelback, and the Papago parks are closed during daylight hours when the forecast exceeds the advisory threshold — a recreation economy learning to schedule around the heat it was built for. A less visible marker is what happens at Sky Harbor.

In June 2017, American Airlines cancelled roughly forty flights at Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport when temperatures hit 118°F. The proximate cause was the operating ceiling for CRJ-series regional jets — a temperature above which the aircraft's performance is not certified. The mechanism is physics: hot air is less dense, generating less lift and less engine thrust; at the limit, some aircraft cannot safely reach takeoff speed within the available runway. Sky Harbor is one of the ten busiest airports in the country. As Phoenix's baseline temperatures rise, the frequency and scope of heat cancellations increases, and the affected aircraft types expand. The airport was not missing from Phoenix's adaptation portfolio. It is being pushed past its design assumptions — which is the definition of stress.

Stress adaptation is not about building new infrastructure. It's about extending what exists. Grid investment in demand flexibility — shifting cooling loads, expanding battery storage, managing peak draw from commercial and industrial users — extends the grid's capacity past where it would otherwise cap out. Cooling cost assistance programs extend households' ability to use the infrastructure they have. Expanded outdoor heat standards — Arizona has no state-level heat safety standard for outdoor workers, which is striking given everything else the state has built — extend protections into sectors and seasons the existing rules didn't reach. Aircraft certification standards and airport operations protocols will need to extend similarly.

The equity question in stress counties is not primarily about access to infrastructure. It's about the compounding cost. Lower-income tracts in Phoenix are hotter than wealthier ones — less canopy, more pavement, older housing stock, less efficient AC equipment. They are also the tracts where a higher share of income goes to cooling, where utility shutoffs hit first, where the financial margin between a bad month and an eviction is narrowest. Stress adaptation that improves the grid and leaves those tracts in the same relative position has not reduced the inequity of heat exposure — it has held it roughly in place while improving conditions for people who were already managing.

## Washtenaw County, Michigan: shift means planning for who's coming

![Washtenaw County heat typology panel](/plots/heat-panel-washtenaw.png)

The tool scores Washtenaw County at 56 percent shift, 44 percent shock — shift-dominant, but with a lower confidence score than Clackamas or Maricopa. That lower confidence is itself informative. Washtenaw is not a place where one trajectory dominates. It's a place in the early stages of a repositioning that the data is only beginning to capture.

Washtenaw County is Ann Arbor, Michigan — home to the University of Michigan, anchored in the Great Lakes watershed, with fresh water access no Sun Belt city can match, winters mild relative to the upper Midwest, and a growing season that is extending as the climate reorganizes. It does not face the wildfire smoke that has become a summer fixture across the West. It does not face coastal flood risk. For residents of Phoenix or the California Central Valley or the Mississippi Delta, it reads, increasingly, as somewhere worth considering.

The tool picks up shift from Washtenaw's climate trajectory: heat days at the 85°F threshold are growing substantially, without proportional growth in the extreme tail. The IRS migration data, which runs through 2023, shows a net outflow of about three percent over five years — the opposite of the in-migration story. That discrepancy is worth sitting with. The migration data captures what has happened; the municipal planning is oriented toward what is coming. Those two things are not yet in the same frame.

Missy Stults, Ann Arbor's sustainability director, has described herself publicly as part of Washtenaw County's "first wave" of climate migrants — someone who relocated in part because of where climate trajectories were heading — and said the county is now seeing its "second or third wave." Her team is having what she calls "really tough conversations" about updating the land use plan, working through how to "best serve existing residents and create space for additional folks to come in in a way that will be successful and without displacement." That last phrase is the equity-of-shift-adaptation argument from Post 2 stated by a practitioner, in real time, in a place that shows up as shift on this tool.

This is a different axis of shift than the agricultural reorganization story in the northern Rockies or the high plains. Washtenaw's shift challenge is not about crop calendars or growing seasons. It's about housing, land use, and whether the planning infrastructure exists to absorb in-migration pressure before it arrives rather than react to it after the fact. What shift places of this kind need is long-horizon planning that most jurisdictions don't have: housing affordability programs sized for anticipated demand rather than current demand, land-use processes that can distinguish between growth that benefits existing residents and growth that displaces them, and institutional capacity to hold those conversations before the pressure peaks rather than during it.

## The line inside shift

There is a nuance the framework doesn't fully resolve, and I want to flag it rather than paper over it.

Shift is not one thing. Washtenaw's shift story is about receiving people displaced from hotter, drier, riskier places — in-migration pressure on housing, on land use, on the social contract between existing and arriving residents. The shift story in the northern Rockies or the high plains is different: local climate reorganization, growing seasons extending, crop viability maps moving north, with the challenge of whether what opens in agriculture compensates for what closes in snowpack and water and winter recreation.

Both are shift. Both share the underlying dynamic of a reordering climate envelope, rather than a hazard newly arriving or an existing system being exceeded. But the response portfolios are different in almost every particular. Washtenaw needs housing policy and land-use planning. A county in eastern Montana needs agricultural extension services, water availability projections, and economic diversification. The tool puts them in the same region of the ternary chart. The practitioner in each place would not recognize the other's problem as the same one.

The tool's ternary chart — which plots every county's position in the three-dimensional score space — suggests this complexity visually. The shift corner contains a lot of territory. There is likely more than one line through it: one separating counties where the reorganization is primarily additive from those where it is primarily subtractive, and another separating counties whose shift is about local climate change from those whose shift is about being on the receiving end of someone else's. Where those lines fall probably depends on domain, on risk tolerance, and on the interaction with other climate stressors — water, fire, smoke — that determine whether what heat opens, other forces close.

That's the version of this framework I'm still working on. The tool as it stands shows the shape. The next version I want to build would show the direction — and the distinction between the two kinds of shift counties that this version has had to hold together.
