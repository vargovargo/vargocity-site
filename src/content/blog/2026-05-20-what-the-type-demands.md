*Three counties, three shapes of heat pressure, three different conversations about what to build next — and a tool to find yours.*

---

The first two posts in this series built the case for a distinction that most climate policy still doesn't make: that a county facing its first real heat emergency is not doing the same thing as one whose cooling infrastructure is being pushed past its design limits, and neither of them is doing the same thing as a county whose entire economic geography is slowly relocating. Shock, stress, shift. Three shapes. Three different responses.

This post tries to make that concrete. The [interactive tool](/lab/heat) assigns each U.S. county a dominant type based on its projected climate trajectory, current exposure, and community context. What follows are three county sketches — one for each type — that try to show what the classification actually implies in practice. Not a scorecard. A starting point for a different conversation.

---

## Clackamas County, Oregon: shock means building what wasn't there

Clackamas County sits south of Portland — suburban in its northwest corner, quickly rural heading east, with small cities that mostly grew up around timber, agriculture, and the mild climate the Pacific Northwest has always offered. In June 2021, its northern neighborhoods recorded temperatures above 110°F. The county's emergency management plan had no heat annex. Most of its housing was built without air conditioning because the climate didn't historically require it. The older stock — much of it rental, much of it upstairs units under flat roofs — had no cross-ventilation, no AC rough-in, no way to stay cool when the outside temperature exceeded the interior even at midnight.

Shock is defined by the absence of prior design. The danger isn't that the cooling system was overwhelmed. It's that there was no cooling system to overwhelm.

What a shock county actually needs is different from what the standard "heat resilience" toolkit usually describes. It needs air conditioning installed in housing where it has never been — not subsidized purchase but subsidized installation, for renters in buildings whose landlords will not act voluntarily. It needs building codes revised to require cooling capacity in new construction, now that the assumption of mild summers no longer holds. It needs emergency management protocols written from scratch for a hazard the jurisdiction has historically treated as a curiosity. It needs outdoor and indoor worker protections extended to a workforce that worked without them because it didn't need them — and then needed them on one week's notice.

Oregon passed its outdoor heat safety rule in 2021, within months of the heat dome, and was first in the country to extend protections to indoor settings as well. The workforce most exposed in those early seasons — agricultural workers, warehouse workers, construction workers — was largely Latino, largely without institutional power to demand protection, and disproportionately housed in exactly the upstairs rentals without AC. The speed of the regulatory response was extraordinary by any standard. It still arrived after the people who died in it.

Shock places have time before the next event — usually, not always — and the investment is in building what the prior assumption said you didn't need. The equity design question is whether the build-out reaches the people without the margin to wait.

## Maricopa County, Arizona: stress means extending past the envelope

Maricopa County knows heat. Phoenix has been managing extreme summer temperature for decades — the built environment is organized around it. The grid is enormous, run hard, and deeply understood. Outdoor work in construction and agriculture has established schedules, shade requirements, hydration stations. The cooling center map exists and is updated annually. The county health department tracks heat-related mortality and has since the 1990s.

What Maricopa is discovering is that the adaptations built for the heat they had are being pushed past the assumptions they were built for.

In 2023, the county recorded 645 confirmed heat-related deaths — a number that involves real undercounting, because death certificates tend to attribute heat illness as a contributing rather than direct cause. The cooling season, once defined by four months of reliably triple-digit temperatures, has been extending into what used to be October and into what used to be April. Nights are warming faster than days. The adaptive significance of that is substantial: the body's capacity to recover from heat stress depends on nighttime temperature dropping far enough for rest. When nights stay in the mid-80s, the recovery window shrinks, and the people who don't have air conditioning in their bedrooms face cumulative heat burden across the whole summer.

Stress adaptation is not about building new infrastructure. It's about extending what exists. Grid investment in demand flexibility — shifting cooling loads, expanding battery storage, managing peak draw from commercial and industrial users — extends the grid's capacity past where it would otherwise cap out. Cooling cost assistance programs extend households' ability to use the infrastructure they have. Expanded outdoor heat standards — Arizona has no state-level heat safety standard for outdoor workers, which is striking — extend protections into sectors and seasons the existing rules didn't reach. Mobile cooling resources for the unsheltered population extend the network of cooling centers to people who can't stay in one location for the day.

The equity question in stress counties is not primarily about access to the infrastructure. It's about the compounding cost. Lower-income tracts in Phoenix are hotter than wealthier ones — less canopy, more pavement, older housing stock, less efficient AC equipment. They are also the tracts where a higher share of income goes to cooling, where utility shutoffs hit first, where the financial margin between a bad month and an eviction is narrowest. Stress adaptation that improves the grid and leaves those tracts in the same relative position has not actually reduced the inequity of heat exposure — it has held it roughly in place while improving conditions for people who were already managing.

## Cascade County, Montana: shift means navigating ambivalence

Cascade County — Great Falls, in north-central Montana — is a different kind of challenge. It's not primarily a story of heat arriving or heat exceeding limits. It's a story of a climate envelope that is reorganizing: growing seasons extending, frost dates shifting, crop viability maps moving north and upward, outdoor work windows that once ended in September lasting later into the fall.

From one angle, that looks like opportunity. Montana's wheat and barley economy has long operated at the edge of viable growing seasons. A longer, warmer window should translate to higher yields — and for some crops and some years, it does. From another angle, it looks like compounding risk: the water that the growing season depends on is a function of snowpack that is also declining, the smoke from wildfires across the mountain West has been blanketing Montana's summers with air quality that limits outdoor work and outdoor recreation, and the same dynamics that are making Montana more attractive to some in-migrants are driving housing costs in ways that existing residents feel sharply.

Shift is the type most resistant to a clean response portfolio, because shift is fundamentally about ambivalence. The same trajectory that opens one window closes another. What's required is not building something new or extending something existing, but repositioning — and repositioning requires a view of where you are going, which requires decisions that communities are only beginning to make.

What shift places need is long-horizon planning infrastructure they typically don't have. Agricultural extension services oriented toward transition — new crop varieties, new planting calendars, water availability under future snowpack projections — rather than optimization of the current regime. Land-use planning that distinguishes between areas gaining long-run viability and areas that are becoming harder to serve. Housing affordability programs that anticipate in-migration pressure before it arrives, rather than chasing it after the fact. Economic diversification that doesn't depend entirely on the winter-recreation and outdoor-season economy that is restructuring under the same forces.

## The line inside shift

There is a nuance the framework doesn't fully resolve, and I want to flag it rather than paper over it.

Not all shift is the same direction. For some counties, the reorganization is primarily additive — new activity becoming viable where it wasn't, with real economic opportunity for people already there. For others, the reorganization is primarily subtractive — activity that was viable no longer being so, with real economic dislocation for people organized around it. The interactive tool clusters both kinds into "shift," because they share the underlying dynamic of a reordering envelope rather than a new or exceeding one. But the response portfolios are meaningfully different.

The tool's ternary chart — which plots every county's position in the three-dimensional score space — suggests this split visually. The shift corner contains a lot of territory. There is likely a line through it, separating counties where the heat trajectory is net-opening from counties where it is net-closing. Where that line falls probably depends on domain (agriculture sees it differently than outdoor recreation), on risk tolerance (a conservative analyst draws it differently than an optimistic one), and on the interaction with other climate stressors — water, fire, smoke — that determine whether what heat opens, water and fire close.

That's the version of this framework I'm still working on. The tool as it stands shows the shape. The next version I want to build would show the direction.
