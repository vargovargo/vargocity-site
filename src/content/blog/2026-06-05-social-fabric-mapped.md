*Where the social fabric is strong, where it's thin, and what the national pattern reveals.*

---

The first post in this series established the framework: social capital is infrastructure, it can be built or allowed to decay, and its collapse is measurable. The second showed that a picture built from public administrative records — library visits, IRS filings, civic organization counts — correlates meaningfully with the behavioral measure Raj Chetty's team derived from billions of Facebook friendships.

This is what that index looks like, mapped across every U.S. county.

![Social fabric map](/plots/social_fabric_map.png)

---

A few things stand out immediately.

The geographic pattern is not noise. The places where civic infrastructure is dense cluster in the upper Midwest and parts of New England — counties with long traditions of civic association, relatively stable institutional density, and moderate population sizes that sustain community life without anonymizing it. The Mountain West and parts of the rural South score consistently lower on the institutional access dimension, reflecting the thinner public institutional infrastructure — fewer libraries per capita, lower nonprofit density — that characterizes low-density, under-resourced counties.

The metro/non-metro split visible in the validation scatter from the previous post resolves into geography here. Metro counties score higher on Civic Life and Institutional Access — denser institutional environments, more cross-group mixing opportunity. Non-metro counties score higher on Community Connections in many regions — rural communities tend toward stronger within-group ties, the bonding dimension that correlates most strongly with lower drug overdose mortality.

What the toggle between dimensions makes visible is that "weak social fabric" is not one thing. A county low on Institutional Access may have dense religious organization and strong community ties. A county low on Community Connections may have robust library infrastructure and civic organization density. The overall score averages across these, but the interventions they point toward are different.

---

The geographic concentration of thin fabric is not incidental. The places where all three dimensions score low simultaneously — low institutional access, low civic life, low community connections — are disproportionately the places that have absorbed the full layering of infrastructure collapse described in the first post: long commutes that eat into the time available for civic participation, physical third-places that closed or were never built, local news ecosystems that have contracted or disappeared entirely.

That compounding is what the causal work in the next post is attempting to isolate — specifically, what happened to the social infrastructure of places that lost their local paper, compared to similar places that didn't. The geographic pattern here is the picture before that analysis. It shows where the fabric is, not yet why.

---

One geographic note: Connecticut appears without data. In 2022, the Census Bureau replaced Connecticut's eight historic counties with nine planning regions for ACS reporting, changing the underlying FIPS codes. The map geometry still uses the old county boundaries. Connecticut's social fabric patterns are present in the underlying data — they just can't be mapped to the old county shapes the map is drawn on.
