*On what happened when technology stopped caring where you live — and what that means for what comes next.*

---

For a brief period, the internet was local.

Not entirely, not primarily — but local in a meaningful way. Community mailing lists, neighborhood forums, local event calendars, online extensions of the civic infrastructure that already existed in physical space. The technology of the late 1990s and early 2000s moved at the pace of the institutions it was adjacent to: the school's parent association email list, the library's online catalog, the city council's meeting agenda page. If you wanted to find out what was happening in your town, you could.

The infrastructure was modest and unglamorous and nobody was particularly proud of it. But it was geographically organized, which meant it was organized around the same unit that all of the other civic infrastructure was organized around: where you actually lived.

The platforms that came next made a different choice. They optimized for attention, not for place. They found that shared outrage traveled farther than local news. That national politics drove more engagement than city council coverage. That recommending content from people who shared your demographic profile was more effective than recommending content from people who shared your zip code. The logic was not hostile to local civic life. It was indifferent to it — indifferent in the way that a financial model is indifferent to what happens to a community when the factory moves. Indifference at scale produces the same outcomes as hostility, and it is much easier to sustain.

---

The local newspaper was the most acute casualty of this shift, but it was not the only one. What closed was not just a distribution channel for local information. It was the institution that built the shared local reality — the thing that made it possible for people who disagreed about everything else to start from a common account of what the city council had voted, what the school board was planning, what the hospital was doing with its land. That shared reality is the informational infrastructure of local civic life, in the same way that the library is the institutional infrastructure and the park is the physical infrastructure. When it closes, what replaces it is not nothing: it is individually curated feeds, engineered for engagement, organized around national identity rather than local one.

---

The civic infrastructure described in the first post in this series — the physical layer of libraries, community centers, and walkable streets; the informational layer of the local press — was all organized, in one way or another, around proximity. It worked because it put people who lived near each other in contact with one another. Not just the people who chose each other. Everyone. The library was not sorted by income or political affiliation. The PTA meeting was not curated to surface only people who already agreed with you. The barbershop was not algorithmically filtered to the customers most likely to engage.

These institutions produced a specific kind of social encounter that is very hard to engineer deliberately: accidental contact across class lines. The firefighter and the architect used the same public pool. The auto mechanic's kid and the professor's kid went to the same public school. These contacts did not automatically produce solidarity or trust or any of the other things associated with strong social fabric. But they produced the preconditions — familiarity, the recognition that the person across from you is a neighbor with a shared stake in the same roads, schools, and water supply.

Algorithmic platforms are designed not to produce that. They surface content from people who share your profile, because shared-profile content produces more engagement. The result is that the digital layer of social life is now more sorted by class, politics, and identity than the physical layer ever was — and people increasingly spend more time in the digital layer.

---

The technology coming next will do this at a different scale. The question is which direction it goes.

At the upper end, AI tools are already qualitatively different from anything that preceded them — capable of doing research, synthesizing information, navigating bureaucratic systems, identifying options that most people would not know to look for. The gap between having access to that and not having access to it is not the gap between a faster phone and a slower one. It is closer to the gap between having a well-connected lawyer on call and representing yourself.

If that technology follows the platform logic — optimizing for engagement and entertainment at the bottom of the market, premium capability at the top — then it will produce a new and far more thorough version of the stratification that platforms produced. Not just different content, but different cognitive and institutional capacity. The places this index describes as most depleted would enter that world with the most to lose and the least to bring.

If it follows a different logic — the logic that built the public library, the community health clinic, the land-grant university — then it could be the contemporary analog to the institutions that drove the early twentieth-century upswing that Putnam documents in *The Upswing*. Deliberate construction, in the places most depleted, of something that substitutes for what is no longer there. The Progressive Era reformers didn't wait for the damage to become legible at scale before they started building. That excuse is no longer available. For the first time, the damage is visible — county by county, dimension by dimension — and that changes what deliberate construction would look like.

---

The research described here is trying to quantify what the collapse of the informational layer has actually cost — so that the choice at this fork is a choice between something and something, not between something and an unknown. The causal work underway will estimate what happened to the social infrastructure of places that lost their local papers, compared to similar places that didn't, before and after the closure. If the link is what the theory predicts, then the cost of the next collapse — the collapse of shared civic capacity at the digital layer — is something the evidence can speak to.

That is what the number is for: not to describe the past, but to clarify what is being decided now.
